v4 costs 37% more to run and delivers 83% more PRs, so the number that matters —
what a merge-ready PR costs — fell by a quarter. The five v2 runs that parked on
defects spent real money and produced nothing mergeable; they were valuable as
findings and worthless as delivery.

**Every fixture passed its `expect` block.** That check is not performed by the
harness — `terminal_status`, `min_sub_tasks` and `requires_pr` are declared at
`eval-run.ts:51-55` and never read — so it was run out-of-band and is the
subject of ATL-138.

### The gates went quiet, and that is the finding

**1 red verdict in 91.** It was real: two `console.log` calls left in
`test/ATL-145.test.js` on ATL-135. `gate-hygiene` failed, the Hygiene Fixer
removed them, `agent-fix-reviewer` confirmed the fix was at the cause, the gate
re-ran green. The whole ADR 0021 loop, exercised exactly once, correctly — and
for ~$0.36 of agent time, against a check that costs nothing when green.

That is the design working. But read the other 90 with ATL-149 in hand:

```
gate-coverage => pass :: skipped - no coverage script declared
gate-visual   => pass :: skipped - @playwright/test is not installed
```

On the sandbox repos, **two of the four gates cannot run at all**, and a skip
and a pass are the same exit code. So "1 of 91 red" overstates how much was
actually verified. The deterministic chain is thinner than the number suggests,
and the one time a red suite reached the end of a run (ATL-110) it was caught by
the Release Reviewer, an LLM, not by a gate.

### Where the money goes

`agent-release-reviewer` is the most expensive agent in the fleet — **$35.34,
$3.21/step, 27% of the total** — on `opus-5[1m]` at `xhigh`. It earned it. Four
rejections across 11 runs, and the two worth naming:

- **ATL-93** — unescaped `${t.id}` reaching rendered HTML. It overruled the
  Architect's "pre-existing and unchanged, out of scope" because the branch
  added the route that made it reachable, reproduced it with two payloads, and
  filed the fix sub-task itself.
- **ATL-110** — the suite was red at HEAD. A doc sub-task had rewritten a README
  line a sibling sub-task's test asserted on. All four gates had just reported
  pass.

Both are cross-sub-task defects. No per-sub-task reviewer could see either,
because in both cases the danger came from one sub-task changing something
another sub-task depended on. That is the argument for the step existing.

`agent-po-writer` is second at $21.99, and 12 of its 12 steps end
`asked_question` by design. Its 11 loops are the answer round — one per answered
run, exactly as intended, with no run needing a second follow-up.

### The v4 routing, on real work

`agent-release-reviewer` rejected 4 times and **no rejection woke the Owner.**
Each filed `dev`-labelled fix sub-tasks, the build step picked them up, and the
chain re-verified through every gate. Under v2 all four would have parked.

`agent-fix-reviewer` ran once, on the only red gate, and passed the fix.

### Judgement the harness cannot score

Three fixtures produced a correct `depends_on` edge without being told to:

```
ATL-123 (dev,fe)  depends_on ATL-121 (dev,be)   contract-split
ATL-151 (dev,fe)  depends_on ATL-150 (dev,be)   greenfield-feature
ATL-166 (dev,be)  depends_on ATL-165 (dev,be)   refactor-no-behaviour-change
```

That is `contract-split`'s entire trap, passed — and the `expect` block cannot
see it. Two escalations from QA agents were the same shape: both refused to
write a test that would contradict either the spec or the suite, and asked
instead. One of them surfaced a conflict between two Owner rulings on different
branches that neither run could have seen on its own.

`pass@1` continues to mis-score this. `agent-release-reviewer` reads **64%**
for the four best calls in the set.
